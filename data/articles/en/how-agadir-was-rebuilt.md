## The decision

Days after [the earthquake](/en/history/agadir-earthquake-1960), King Mohammed V visited the ruins and made the pledge that defined a generation: Agadir would be rebuilt. The decision that followed was harder and braver — **not to rebuild on the bones**. The new city centre was planned around two kilometres south of the destroyed one, on ground clear of the fault, and the ruined site was left largely untouched. To this day, unrecovered dead rest beneath the Oufella hill.

## A laboratory of modernism

The reconstruction turned Agadir into one of the boldest urban experiments of the twentieth century. Architects came from across Morocco and abroad — including the modernists of the GAMMA group, **Jean-François Zevaco, Elie Azagury and Mourad Ben Embarek**, alongside Pierre Coldefy and Claude Verdugo.

What they built was guided by three rules:

- **Seismic safety first.** The new Agadir produced North Africa's first serious seismic construction codes — the ancestors of the standards (today the RPS 2000 code) that saved lives in the 2023 Marrakesh earthquake.
- **Openness.** Wide boulevards and low, spaced blocks — emergency access and safe assembly zones turned into urban design.
- **Function and climate.** Reinforced concrete, flat roofs, deep overhangs and brise-soleil sun-breakers handling the Atlantic light.

Zevaco's **central post office (1960)** and fire station became icons of what is sometimes called "Gadirian" modernism, and his 1965 courtyard housing later earned the Aga Khan Award for Architecture.

## From port town to beach capital

The new master plan gave the coast to tourism, and tourism repaid the city:

- **1970s–80s:** the hotel strip and promenade rise; Agadir becomes Morocco's premier beach resort
- **1992:** the Jardin d'Olhão opens, honouring Agadir's friendship with its Portuguese sister city
- **1990s:** the Medina d'Agadir — a traditional medina recreated from scratch by the Italian artist Coco Polizzi, in dialogue with the old city that can never return
- **2020s:** the marina, the cable car to the Oufella hill, and record tourism — **more than 1.5 million arrivals in 2025**, with African Cup of Nations matches played in the city

## Why the city looks the way it does

Visitors sometimes say Agadir "doesn't feel like Morocco" — no tangled medieval medina, no ancient gates. That is not a lack. It is a scar that healed: the old city died in fifteen seconds, and what you walk today is a deliberate, engineered answer to that night.

Every wide boulevard is seismic safety turned into urbanism. Every low skyline is a promise to the dead. And at the centre of the rebuilt city beats its commercial heart — [the souk that the earthquake exiled and could not kill](/en/history/from-talborjt-to-amsernat).

> Stand on the Oufella hill at sunset, look south across the white city — then walk down into Souk Al Ahad. That single walk is the whole story of Agadir.
