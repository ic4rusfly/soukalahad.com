## The night Agadir fell

At around 23:40 on the night of **29 February 1960** — in the middle of Ramadan — the ground under Agadir moved for roughly **fifteen seconds**. The magnitude was moderate, about 5.8, but two details made it catastrophic: the epicentre lay almost underneath the city, and the rupture was shallow. A moderate quake, directly beneath a sleeping city, is the worst combination in seismology.

Agadir in 1960 was a fast-growing port and beach resort of some 40,000–50,000 people. A 16th-century kasbah — **Agadir Oufella** — stood on the hill above the port. Below it spread the modern "new town" of boulevards and hotels, and between them the dense traditional quarters: **Talborjt, Founty and Yachech**.

## What fifteen seconds did

**More than 90% of the buildings in the central districts collapsed.** The kasbah was destroyed. Talborjt — the old heart of town — was hit hardest. The hospital, the new luxury Hôtel Saada and the National Militia headquarters all came down. Fire spread through the ruins in the hours that followed.

The human toll remains hard to state precisely, because whole families and whole archives vanished together:

- An estimated **12,000–15,000 dead** — roughly a third of the city's population
- Around **25,000 injured**
- Between **35,000 and 50,000 homeless**

The poor construction of the traditional quarters — unreinforced adobe and masonry, built with no seismic standards at all — is what turned fifteen seconds into a mass grave.

## Rescue under the rubble

The airport, almost undamaged, became a field hospital; the wounded were flown to Casablanca through the night. Rescue teams arrived from across Morocco and from around the world. Survivors — including children — were still being pulled alive from the rubble **up to twelve days** later.

Two days after the quake, the authorities ordered the **total evacuation** of what remained of the city, fearing disease.

## The hill that remembers

On the Oufella hill today, the outer wall of the old kasbah has been restored, and the view over the port and the new city is magnificent. But the hill itself was deliberately left as a memorial — there are still unrecovered dead beneath it. Stand there at sunset and you are standing on the grave of the old Agadir.

The 1960 earthquake remains the deadliest in Morocco's recorded history. (The magnitude-6.9 Al Haouz earthquake near Marrakesh in September 2023 killed far fewer people precisely because Morocco rebuilt on the lessons of Agadir.)
